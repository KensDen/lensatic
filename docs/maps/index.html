01

Start here: the trailheads

For anyone handed a framework, a maturity model, or a zero trust deadline and asked where it fits: federal civilian agencies, Department of War (DoW) components, defense contractors, and the state and local governments, companies and universities that borrow the United States federal models. Start at the trailhead that describes you.

Zero trust, the idea most of these documents serve, means nothing is trusted because of where it connects from: every request to reach data or a system is checked against policy each time.

Department of War ComponentYou are a DoW component, or a program that reports to one, working toward Target Level zero trust.7 steps · layers 1, 3, 4, 5

Steps

  1. Layer 3 Start with the DoD (Department of Defense) Zero Trust Strategy and its execution roadmap, which set the Target Level, the deadline, and the phasing; the implementing memorandum, directive-type memorandum (DTM) 25-003, passed its printed expiry on 17 July 2026 with no replacement found, so the FY2027 (fiscal year) deadline rests on those two documents. Keep your component’s zero trust implementation plan current and ask the zero trust portfolio office what it expects now: the strategy set the first plans due to the DoW CIO (chief information officer) and JFHQ-DODIN (Joint Force Headquarters-Department of Defense Information Network), now the Cyber Defense Command, by 23 September 2023, and no current source repeats the yearly submission the DTM required. DoD ZT Strategy, DoD ZT Roadmap
  2. Layer 3 Work the seven pillars and 45 capabilities as your model; each capability breaks into activities, 152 in all, and the 91 at Target Level come first, with the 61 at Advanced Level where the mission needs them; the roadmap offers three courses of action (uplift your own infrastructure, use a commercial cloud, or use an on-premises cloud the department or a component owns) and lets you mix them, and an enterprise service can deliver some activities for you (see Questions). DoD ZT Strategy, DoD ZT Roadmap
  3. ZT for OT
  4. Layer 3 Use the National Security Agency (NSA) information sheets for each pillar and the 2026 implementation guidelines to sequence the activities. NSA ZT guidance
  5. Layer 4 Apply the DoD Zero Trust Overlays to select the SP (Special Publication) 800-53 controls that implement each capability; the overlays are not posted publicly in 2026, so ask your component or the zero trust portfolio office for the current copy. DoD ZT Overlays, SP 800-53
  6. DoD ZT Strategy, RMF / ATO, CORA

Federal contract information only

Federal contract information is information not intended for public release that is provided by or generated for the government under a contract. If you hold only federal contract information for any agency, the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) basic safeguarding clause, 52.204-21 (52.240-93 in contracts written under the FAR overhaul deviations), sets your 15 requirements, and on a DoW contract that carries the Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC) clause, Level 1 is a self-assessment against those same 15. Nothing else on this stack binds you; use CSF 2.0 to talk about the rest. CMMC, CSF 2.0

Controlled unclassified information for a civilian agency

Controlled unclassified information is government information that is not classified but that a law, regulation or government-wide policy requires or permits agencies to safeguard or control. If you hold controlled unclassified information for a civilian agency, your contract clause names the requirements, normally a revision of SP 800-171, and the assessment the clause specifies proves them; CMMC does not apply. Use SP 800-207 for what zero trust means, the CISA maturity model if the agency asks for a target, and CSF 2.0 to tell the agency where you stand. SP 800-171, SP 800-207, CISA ZTMM, CSF 2.0

02

The stack

The stack sorts the frameworks by the question each one answers: five layers, from the outcomes leadership funds down to who checks the evidence, with an AI column beside them. Solid chips are frameworks, dashed chips are other guides and memos placed on a layer, and dotted chips are solutions measured against a layer.

SP (Special Publication) 800-207 defines zero trust architecture: no implicit trust from network location, and every request decided by a policy engine and then allowed or blocked at a policy enforcement point. CSF (Cybersecurity Framework) 2.0 gives an organization six functions, Profiles, and Tiers to describe cybersecurity outcomes in language leadership funds against. SP 800-53 is the catalog of security and privacy controls that federal systems select from, and that CSF references and the zero trust overlays both point into. The AI RMF (Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework) gives an organization four functions, Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage, to handle the risks of AI systems in outcome language. The Cyber AI Profile is a draft CSF 2.0 Community Profile that crosses the six functions with three focus areas: securing AI system components, conducting AI-enabled cyber defense, and thwarting AI-enabled cyber attacks. The AI Defense Matrix lays the six CSF 2.0 functions across eight AI-specific asset classes, from models and training data to agent identities, the same two-axis move the zero trust matrix makes.

Questions: which framework, when

Is CSF (Cybersecurity Framework) 2.0 mandatory?

Not by its own force. CSF 2.0 is voluntary by design, and Executive Order 13800 makes each federal agency head, civilian and Department of War (DoW), use it. For federal agencies the mandatory engine underneath is the RMF (Risk Management Framework) and SP (Special Publication) 800-53 under FISMA (Federal Information Security Modernization Act), so the CSF is the communication and prioritization layer above the controls. Its Informative References map each outcome down to the controls.

Draws on: CSF 2.0, RMF / ATO, SP 800-53, EO 13800

How does CSF 2.0 differ from ISO/IEC 27001 (International Organization for Standardization and International Electrotechnical Commission)?

CSF 2.0 is an outcomes framework; ISO/IEC 27001 is a certifiable management system standard. They are complementary: an organization can hold an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate and still use a CSF Profile to talk about outcomes, and the Informative References cross-map the two. NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology)’s Informative Reference Catalog lists an ISO/IEC 27001:2022 to CSF 2.0 reference.

Draws on: CSF 2.0

What do we do first?

If a mandate binds you, your trailhead’s first step names it, and the trailhead’s later steps supply the target. In CSF 2.0 terms: scope the Profile and gather what you need, then start with the Govern outcomes: organizational context, risk strategy, and roles. Build a Current Profile and a Target Profile, analyze the gaps and plan, then implement and update. The action plan comes from the gap between the two Profiles.

Draws on: CSF 2.0

How does CSF 2.0 relate to zero trust?

SP 800-207 defines zero trust, and the zero trust models supply the pillars and the maturity ladder. CSF 2.0 does not define zero trust; it gives you the outcome language. Put your model’s pillars as columns and the six functions as rows, fill the matrix, and the result is a zero trust Target Profile: leadership reads it in outcome language, and the Informative References under the Subcategories of each cell’s Categories carry it down to SP 800-53 or your own control catalog. It also puts zero trust in Govern, Identify, and Detect, not only in Protect. Maturity stays with the zero trust model you use; CSF 2.0 Tiers characterize the rigor of an organization’s cybersecurity risk governance and management practices.

Draws on: SP 800-207, CSF 2.0, CISA ZTMM, DoD ZT Strategy, SP 800-53

Where are the mappings?

On the NIST site: the CSF 2.0 Reference Tool and the Informative Reference Catalog map each Subcategory to SP 800-53 and other sources. For either pillar model, each matrix cell carries CSF 2.0 categories; the Subcategories under those categories carry the Informative References to SP 800-53; the CISA (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency) model prints no control mapping of its own. For the DoW pillars, the DoD (Department of Defense) Zero Trust Overlays map each capability to SP 800-53 controls; they are not posted publicly in 2026, so a DoW component asks its own leadership or the zero trust portfolio office for the current copy, and anyone borrowing the pillars works from the capability list and the catalog. NIST’s practice guide on implementing a zero trust architecture, SP 1800-35 (final, June 2025), also publishes mappings of zero trust architecture principles and technologies to commonly used security standards and guidelines; they map its example builds, not the CISA or DoW pillar models.

Draws on: CSF 2.0, SP 800-53, DoD ZT Overlays, CISA ZTMM, SP 1800-35

Where do SP 800-171, CMMC (Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification), FedRAMP (Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program), ISO/IEC 27001, CIS (Center for Internet Security) Controls, STIGs (security technical implementation guides), IL4 (impact level) through IL6, and MITRE ATT&CK (Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge) land?

SP 800-171 and CMMC are the contractor trailhead: a requirements baseline and the assessment that proves it, layers 4 and 5. FedRAMP is the governmentwide program that certifies cloud services for agency use, so it is verification (layer 5); its Rev5 path assesses an SP 800-53 baseline (layer 4), and its 20x path, widely available since June 2026, assesses Key Security Indicators instead. ISO/IEC 27001 sits beside CSF 2.0 as a management system standard. CIS Controls are a control catalog, layer 4. STIGs and SRGs (security requirements guides) are the DoW configuration baseline at layer 4. IL4 through IL6 are DoW cloud impact levels that set the baseline and the authorization bar, layers 4 and 5 again. ATT&CK is not a layer: it is the map of adversary behavior the stack defends against, most useful under Detect and Respond, and CORA (Cyber Operational Readiness Assessment) scores against its tactics.

Draws on: SP 800-171, CMMC, SP 800-53, RMF / ATO, CORA, CSF 2.0

Where do validated solutions and enterprise services land?

They are not frameworks. A solution such as Thunderdome or Flank Speed is an implementation vehicle measured against the layer 3 activities: an assessment tests which of the DoW zero trust activities the service delivers. An organization that adopts one may inherit some of those activities, not all of them (inherit is this app’s word: the DoW activities table splits each activity between enterprise and component responsibility instead), and the way to say which are inherited and which are its own is an activity-level instrument: a record, activity by activity, of what the solution delivers and what the organization still does.

Draws on: Thunderdome, Flank Speed, DoD ZT Strategy, DoD ZT Roadmap, ZT for OT, CISA ZTMM, NSA ZT guidance

Where does operational technology land, and where does IEC 62443 fit?

Operational technology (OT) lands at layer 3. On the DoW side it has its own baseline: activities and outcomes that adapt the enterprise zero trust activities to OT and control systems under the same seven pillars, stay aligned with the enterprise IT (information technology) activities, and are implemented in addition to existing cybersecurity requirements; the document says separate guidance will be developed for weapon systems and defense critical infrastructure. Civilian agencies have no separate OT baseline on this stack: the CISA maturity model is still their model, CISA’s 2026 companion, Adapting Zero Trust Principles to Operational Technology, applies it to OT, and NIST SP 800-82 Revision 3 is NIST’s guide to OT security. IEC 62443, from the International Electrotechnical Commission, is a series of standards for the security of industrial automation and control systems, not a zero trust model: the DoW document names it, with the Purdue Model, among the established reference architectures it treats as authoritative for classifying OT systems.

Draws on: ZT for OT, DoD ZT Strategy, CISA ZTMM

Glossary

Every abbreviation Framework Maps uses, spelled out, with a plain sentence on what it is. Each spelling was checked against its publisher; an entry carries a badge only where that could not be confirmed. Each section also spells out an abbreviation where it first appears, except AI, which every reader knows. The playbook section keeps its own glossary of AI security terms, in its AI Fluency tab.

AI
artificial intelligence. In the AI Risk Management Framework from NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology), an AI system is an engineered or machine-based system that can, for a given set of objectives, generate outputs such as predictions, recommendations or decisions. See: AI RMF
AI RMF
Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework. NIST’s framework for managing the risks that artificial intelligence poses to individuals, organizations and society. See: AI RMF
ATO
authorization to operate. A senior official’s decision to let an information system operate and to accept the risk that remains. See: RMF / ATO
ATT&CK
Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge. MITRE’s public catalog of the tactics and techniques attackers use, built from observed attacks; the long form is printed in a 2017 MITRE technical report, and MITRE now uses ATT&CK as a registered trademark.
C3PAO
CMMC (Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification) Third-Party Assessment Organization. An organization authorized or accredited to conduct CMMC Level 2 certification assessments. See: CMMC
CC BY
Creative Commons Attribution. An open license that lets anyone share and adapt the material for any purpose, even commercially, provided they give appropriate credit, link to the license and say if they made changes; Lensatic’s own text was released under it through content version 0.6.0.
CC BY-SA
Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike. An open license that allows reuse with credit, provided anyone who distributes an adaptation uses the same license. See: AI Defense Matrix
CFR
Code of Federal Regulations. The codification of the general and permanent rules that federal departments and agencies publish in the Federal Register. See: CMMC
CIO
chief information officer. The senior official responsible for an organization’s information technology; the department’s CIO publishes its zero trust strategy. See: DoD ZT Strategy
CIS
Center for Internet Security. A nonprofit that publishes the CIS Controls and the CIS Benchmarks, guidance for securing computer systems and data.
CISA
Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. The federal agency that works with partners to defend against cyber threats; it published the civilian Zero Trust Maturity Model. See: CISA ZTMM
CMMC
Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification. The department’s program for verifying that contractors meet the security requirements for the information they handle. See: CMMC
COA
course of action. One of the three routes the department’s zero trust roadmap offers: uplifting legacy systems, commercial cloud, or purpose-built on-premise cloud. See: DoD ZT Roadmap
Community Profile
A baseline of CSF (Cybersecurity Framework) outcomes created and published to address shared interests and goals among a number of organizations, typically for a sector, technology or threat type. See: CSF 2.0, Cyber AI Profile
CORA
Cyber Operational Readiness Assessment. The department’s readiness assessment of its networks; in 2024 its former cyber readiness inspection program was reworked and renamed CORA. See: CORA
CoSAI
Coalition for Secure AI. An OASIS Open Project in which experts from many organizations write guidance for securing AI systems. See: CoSAI ZT for AI
CSF
Cybersecurity Framework. NIST’s taxonomy of high-level cybersecurity outcomes that any organization can use to understand, assess, prioritize and communicate its cybersecurity efforts. See: CSF 2.0
CSRC
Computer Security Resource Center. NIST’s website for its computer security publications, projects and glossary.
CSRMC
Cybersecurity Risk Management Construct. An approach to cybersecurity risk in five phases that the Department of War (DoW) CIO announced on 24 September 2025 and presents as the successor to the RMF process. See: RMF / ATO
CSWP
Cybersecurity White Paper. A NIST publication series; CSF 2.0 was published as CSWP 29. See: CSF 2.0
CUI
Controlled Unclassified Information. Government information that is not classified but that a law, regulation or government-wide policy requires or permits agencies to safeguard or control. See: SP 800-171
DCDC
Department of Defense Cyber Defense Command. The U.S. (United States) Cyber Command component that leads network operations, security and defense across the department’s information network; its site now also uses the name Department of War Cyber Defense Command. See: CORA
DCMA
Defense Contract Management Agency. The department agency that works directly with defense suppliers so the government receives quality products on time and at projected cost. See: CMMC
DCSA
Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency. The department agency that vets the federal and contractor workforce and oversees industrial security for cleared contractors; it also runs cyber operational readiness assessments of contractors with approved SIPRNet (Secret Internet Protocol Router Network) systems. See: CORA
DFARS
Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement. The department’s implementation of, and supplement to, the Federal Acquisition Regulation, including the contract clauses on contractor cybersecurity. See: CMMC, SP 800-171
DIB
Defense Industrial Base. The U.S. and allied companies and organizations that research, design, produce, deliver and maintain the department’s systems and parts. See: CMMC
DIBCAC
Defense Industrial Base Cybersecurity Assessment Center. The Defense Contract Management Agency center that assesses contractors’ compliance with DFARS cybersecurity clauses and NIST SP (Special Publication) 800-171, and the only body designated to assess CMMC Level 3. See: CMMC
DISA
Defense Information Systems Agency. The department agency that plans, builds and operates information-sharing capabilities and a global enterprise infrastructure; it runs Thunderdome. See: Thunderdome
DLP
data loss prevention. A system’s ability to identify, monitor and protect data in use, in motion and at rest. See: DoD ZT Roadmap
DoD
Department of Defense. The department’s name in law; titles of documents issued under it are shown here as printed, with DoD. See: DoD ZT Strategy
DOI
Digital Object Identifier. A persistent identifier for a publication or other object; NIST technical publications carry one. See: SP 1800-35
DON
Department of the Navy. The military department for the Navy and Marine Corps; a joint memorandum from its CIO designated Flank Speed’s productivity and messaging services an enterprise IT service. See: Flank Speed
DoW
Department of War. The secondary name the department has used since September 2025; its title in law is still Department of Defense.
DTM
directive-type memorandum. A department issuance that establishes or implements policy, assigns responsibilities and may provide procedures. See: DoD ZT Strategy
EDR
endpoint detection and response. Tools that monitor, detect and remediate malicious activity on endpoints. See: DoD ZT Roadmap
EO
Executive Order. A directive from the President to the executive branch, published in the Federal Register. See: EO 14028, EO 13800
FAR
Federal Acquisition Regulation. The primary document of the system of uniform policies and procedures for acquisition by all executive agencies; agency regulations such as DFARS implement or supplement it.
FCI
Federal contract information. Information not intended for public release that is provided by or generated for the government under a contract. See: CMMC
FedRAMP
Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program. The governmentwide program that provides a standardized approach to security and risk assessment for cloud products and services.
FISMA
Federal Information Security Modernization Act. The federal law, first enacted in 2002 as the Federal Information Security Management Act and amended in 2014 by the Federal Information Security Modernization Act, that requires each agency to develop, document and implement an agency-wide information security program. See: CSF 2.0
FR
Federal Register. The official daily publication of federal rules, proposed rules, notices and executive orders, cited by volume and page, as in 86 FR 26633.
FY
fiscal year. The federal budget year, which begins on 1 October and ends on 30 September of the following year, as in FY2027.
GPS
Global Positioning System. The United States-owned utility that provides positioning, navigation and timing services; the U.S. Space Force operates and maintains it.
H.R.
House of Representatives. The prefix on a bill introduced in the House, as in H.R. 8800; the number stays with the bill through every stage.
IBM
International Business Machines. The technology company that designed the IBM Plex typefaces Lensatic is set in and holds their copyright.
ICAM
identity, credential and access management. The programs, processes, technologies and people used to create trusted digital identities, bind them to credentials, and use those credentials for access. See: DoD ZT Roadmap
IEC
International Electrotechnical Commission. The international body that writes standards for electrical and electronic technology, including the IEC 62443 series. See: ZT for OT
IL
impact level. The department’s cloud security levels, set by how sensitive the information is and the potential impact if it is compromised.
IR
incident response. The remediation or mitigation of violations of security policies and recommended practices. See: DoD ZT Roadmap
ISO
International Organization for Standardization. An international standards organization.
IT
information technology. Equipment and systems used to acquire, store, process and move data or information.
JFHQ-DODIN
Joint Force Headquarters-Department of Defense Information Network. The command that launched CORA in 2024, since renamed the Cyber Defense Command. See: CORA
MDM
mobile device management. The administration of mobile devices such as smartphones, tablets and laptops, usually through a third-party product. See: DoD ZT Roadmap
MFA
multi-factor authentication. Proving identity, for a user or a non-person entity, with at least two of three factors: something you know, something you have, or something you are. See: DoD ZT Roadmap
MIT License

MITRE
The organization that maintains ATT&CK, a public catalog of the tactics and techniques attackers use.
NAVADMIN
Navy specific administrative message. An administrative message specific to the Navy, as the Navy glosses the term; one sets out who leads migration onto Flank Speed. See: Flank Speed
NCCoE
National Cybersecurity Center of Excellence. A NIST hub where industry, government and academia work together on businesses’ most pressing cybersecurity challenges; it produced the zero trust practice guide. See: SP 1800-35
NDAA
National Defense Authorization Act. The law, passed for each fiscal year, that authorizes appropriations for the Department of Defense and sets defense policies.
NIST
National Institute of Standards and Technology. A U.S. Department of Commerce agency, founded in 1901, that publishes the Cybersecurity Framework and the Special Publications cited here. See: CSF 2.0
NIST IR
NIST Internal Report. A NIST report series; the Cyber AI Profile draft is NIST IR 8596. See: Cyber AI Profile
NSA
National Security Agency. The agency that leads U.S. government cryptology, including cybersecurity products and services; it publishes zero trust guidance for owners of National Security Systems, department networks and the defense industrial base. See: NSA ZT guidance
OASIS Open
A nonprofit standards body that hosts open projects, CoSAI among them. See: CoSAI ZT for AI
OFL
SIL Open Font License. A free license for typefaces, published by SIL: anyone may use, change and share the fonts, including inside software, but may not sell the fonts on their own; the typefaces Lensatic uses are under version 1.1.
OMB
Office of Management and Budget. The White House office whose memoranda direct federal agencies, including the zero trust memo M-22-09. See: M-22-09, M-25-21
OT
operational technology. Programmable systems and devices that interact with the physical environment, such as industrial control and building automation systems. See: ZT for OT
PAM
privileged access management. How an organization controls, monitors, secures and audits privileged identities, for example through password vaulting. See: DoD ZT Roadmap
PDF
Portable Document Format. A file format that represents formatted, page-oriented documents.
PDP
policy decision point. The part of a zero trust system that computes access decisions by evaluating the applicable policies. See: DoD ZT Roadmap, SP 800-207
PfMO
Portfolio Management Office. The office in the department CIO’s organization that provides strategic guidance, aligns efforts and prioritizes resources to accelerate zero trust adoption. See: DoD ZT Strategy
PMO
program management office. The department CIO’s CMMC office, which oversees the CMMC program, including its accreditation body. See: CMMC
RMF
Risk Management Framework. NIST’s process that builds security, privacy and cyber supply chain risk management into a system’s development life cycle. See: RMF / ATO
S.
Senate. The prefix on a bill introduced in the Senate, as in S. 4784; the number follows the order in which bills are introduced.
SDN
software-defined networking. Programmable network infrastructure that separates the control and data planes so the network can be centrally managed. See: DoD ZT Roadmap
SIEM
security information and event management. A program that provides centralized logging for many types of logs. See: DoD ZT Roadmap
SIL
The language-development organization, now SIL Global, that publishes the SIL Open Font License.
SIPRNet
Secret Internet Protocol Router Network. The department’s classified network for moving secret information, provided as a DISA transport service. See: CORA
SOAR
security orchestration, automation and response. Tools that ingest alert data and trigger automated response and remediation to speed up security operations and incident response. See: DoD ZT Roadmap
SOC
security operations center. The unit that runs an organization’s security operations; the AI column’s worked example is a Profile for an AI-enabled one.
SP
Special Publication. A NIST publication series; the SP 800 documents cover computer security. See: SP 800-53
SPRS
Supplier Performance Risk System. The system in which the department sees contractors’ SP 800-171 assessment scores and their CMMC status; under DFARS 252.204-7019 an offeror that must meet SP 800-171 needs a current score posted there to be considered for award. See: SP 800-171, CMMC
SRG
security requirements guide. A compilation of security requirements grouped by technology area and product specificity. See: SP 800-53
STIG
security technical implementation guide. An implementation guide for securing a specific product and version, based on department policy and security controls. See: SP 800-53
U.S.
United States. The abbreviation for United States, as in U.S. Government.
U.S.C.
United States Code. The consolidation and codification, by subject matter, of the general and permanent laws of the United States.
UEM
unified endpoint management. A centralized solution that manages computers and mobile devices, with or without an agent, from a single console. See: DoD ZT Roadmap
UFC
Unified Facilities Criteria. Department of Defense criteria for the planning, design, construction, sustainment, restoration and modernization of facilities; UFC 4-010-06 covers the cybersecurity of facility-related control systems. See: ZT for OT
URL
uniform resource locator. An identifier that names a resource and also says how to locate it, as a web page’s address does.
XDR
extended detection and response. Detection and response tooling that extends beyond endpoints to activity such as cloud and network. See: DoD ZT Roadmap
ZIG
Zero Trust Implementation Guidelines. NSA’s series of guides that set out steps to reach the department’s Target Level zero trust capabilities, activities and outcomes. See: NSA ZT guidance
ZT
zero trust. A security model in which nothing is trusted because of where it connects from, and every request is checked against policy. See: SP 800-207, DoD ZT Strategy
ZTA
zero trust architecture. A plan that uses zero trust principles to lay out an organization’s industrial and enterprise infrastructure and workflows. See: SP 800-207
ZTMM
Zero Trust Maturity Model. CISA’s model for measuring a civilian agency’s zero trust progress in five pillars and four stages. See: CISA ZTMM
